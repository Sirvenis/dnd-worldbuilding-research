# Session Zero: Best Practices for D&D Campaigns

> *Research compiled for the D&D World-Building Research Repository.*
> *Sources: D&D Beyond, Sly Flourish, Dungeon Dudes (Drakkenheim), Threadfall, D&D Rules, Roll20 Compendium (2024 DMG), Golden Lasso Games, and TTRPG community resources.*

---

## What Is a Session Zero?

A **Session Zero** is a dedicated pre-campaign meeting held before any actual gameplay begins. It is a consent-and-calibration exercise, not a gameplay session. The goal is to replace assumptions with agreements—to answer four core questions together, out loud, before play begins:

1. **What kind of game are we playing?**
2. **What tone and content are acceptable (and what's off-limits)?**
3. **How will we handle logistics—scheduling, absences, phones, snacks?**
4. **Who are these characters, and why are they together?**

The concept originated in the D&D community and has become so widespread that it was included for the first time in the **2024 Dungeon Master's Guide** (Wizards of the Coast). Session Zero is now recognised as the cheapest insurance in the hobby: two hours of alignment now can save a campaign that would otherwise die at session eight.

> **Golden Rule:** Session Zero builds a **party**, not just characters.

---

## Recommended Duration

| Group Type | Suggested Duration |
|------------|-------------------|
| Experienced players who know each other | 90 minutes – 2 hours |
| Standard mixed-experience group | 2–3 hours |
| New players / first-time group | 2.5–3 hours (split into two sittings if needed) |
| One-shot or mini-campaign | 30–45 minutes, condensed into the start of session one |

A table meeting for the first time often runs better split into two sittings: the talk first, then a short starter mission within a week once characters are finished.

## The Seven Pillars of Session Zero

### 1. Campaign Pitch and World Introduction

The DM describes the campaign's premise, genre, tone, expected length, and the world's core truths. Players should leave knowing what they signed up for, not discovering it at session five.

**What to cover:**
- **Elevator pitch:** One to three sentences capturing the campaign's hook.
- **Setting:** Forgotten Realms, Eberron, homebrew, or a published adventure (e.g., Drakkenheim).
- **Core truths:** Six truths known to the characters that set this campaign apart from all others. These are not secrets—they are what the characters already know about the world.
- **Campaign length:** Rough session count or open-ended.
- **Pre-made adventure disclosure:** If running published content, ask if anyone has played or read it before.

**Drakkenheim Example:**
The Dungeon Dudes explicitly recommend sharing the intro cinematic, Chapter 1 of the book (spoiler-free), and the world appendix with players before Session Zero. They lay the city map out on the table immediately—familiarising players with locations like Castle Drakken, the Academy Tower, and Slaughter Stone Square builds excitement and investment.

> **Drakkenheim's Pitch:** "A dark fantasy cosmic horror campaign set in a ruined city ravaged by civil war and a mysterious meteor. Expect moral ambiguity, faction intrigue, and dangerous monsters. This is not a 'unite the factions to save the world' story—your choices will make enemies as well as allies."

---

### 2. Tone and Genre Calibration

Tone mismatches are the most common cause of campaign failure. One player expects somber, consequence-heavy drama; another expects comedic chaos. Neither is wrong, but they produce incompatible behaviour.

**Discuss explicitly:**
- Seriousness vs. comedy (and the ratio between them)
- Heroic vs. gritty vs. grimdark
- Moral complexity: black-and-white vs. ambiguous
- Horror elements: body horror, psychological, cosmic, or none
- Romance and mature content
- Consequence level: cartoonish villains vs. tragic, complex ones
- Reference points: "Think The Princess Bride with more goblins" or "Dark Souls meets Renaissance intrigue"

**The "Three Adjectives, Sight Unseen" Technique:**
Each player writes three adjectives for the game they want, without seeing the others'. Then all are read aloud. Concrete words surface a mismatch faster than "what tone do you want?" ever does.

### 3. Safety Tools: Lines, Veils, and the X-Card

Safety tools are not distrust—they are the mechanism that lets friends play intense games safely. Even tables of long-standing friends benefit from them, because friends can still surprise each other.

#### Lines (Hard Limits)
**Lines** are hard stops: topics that will **not appear in this campaign at all**. If a player has a genuine aversion to something, it goes on the list and you write around it entirely.

Common lines include:
- Physical violence toward children or animals
- Sexual assault or unwanted sexual contact
- Torture (player-initiated)
- Inter-party violence or betrayal
- Racism, slavery, or specific phobias (spiders, snakes, etc.)

#### Veils (Soft Limits)
**Veils** are softer: the content can exist in the fiction but stays **off-camera**. The event happens; we just don't narrate it.

Common veils include:
- Cannibalism
- Suffocation
- Ritual sacrifice
- Consensual sex (fade to black)
- Enemy-initiated torture
- Detailed gore or body horror

#### The X-Card
The **X-Card** is a physical card or verbal signal any player can use to skip or edit a scene. **No explanation required. No questions asked.** The scene shifts. It is a circuit breaker for a single moment, not a veto on the campaign.

> **Best Practice:** Ask about lines and veils **privately** before Session Zero. Some players won't speak up in a group. A DM message three days before ("Here's the pitch—any content that's a hard pass for you?") surfaces more than any group conversation.

#### Script Change (Advanced Safety Tool)
Designed by Beau Jágr Sheldon, **Script Change** uses film-editing metaphors:
- **Rewind:** Take the game back to before uncomfortable content; retcon as needed.
- **Pause:** Put the scene on hold. Take a breath. Discuss if needed.
- **Fast Forward:** Skip past uncomfortable content to a logical conclusion.
- **Frame-by-Frame:** Slow down a scene to check in with everyone.

Anyone can call a Script Change verbally or by raising/tapping a card. No explanation is required. After resolving, anyone can say **"resume"** to continue.

#### The Open-Door Rule
Any player can step away from the table at any time for any reason. The table doesn't make it weird.

### 4. Game Rules and House Rules

Surprise rulings mid-session feel arbitrary. Agreed-on house rules feel like shared design.

**Document explicitly:**
- **Edition:** D&D 5e (2014 or 2024 rules), Pathfinder, etc.
- **Stat generation:** Standard Array, Point Buy, or rolled (and if rolled, where and when)
- **Allowed sourcebooks:** PHB only, or includes Xanathar's, Tasha's, third-party?
- **Homebrew / UA:** What's permitted and what isn't
- **Multiclassing:** Allowed or restricted
- **Feats:** Allowed? Restricted?
- **Optional rules:** Flanking, critical fumbles, lingering injuries, etc.
- **Experience:** Milestone levelling, XP-for-combat, or story-based advancement
- **Death and resurrection:** How lethal is the game? What happens when a character dies?
- **Inspiration:** How is it earned and used?
- **Resting:** Standard rules, gritty realism, or custom?
- **Metagaming policy:** How is player knowledge vs. character knowledge handled?
- **DM authority:** Does the DM have final say, or is RAW (Rules as Written) gospel?

**Drakkenheim-Specific Rules to Flag:**
- **The Haze:** A magical radiation covering the city. Characters need to understand contamination mechanics upfront.
- **Encounter balance is not guaranteed:** The city is dangerous; running away is always valid.
- **Contamination:** Eldritch corruption that can physically and mentally mutate characters.
- **Open-world danger:** Areas are not level-gated. Low-level characters can wander into lethal situations.

### 5. Character Creation and Party Glue

The mistake most GMs make is letting players design characters alone at home, then bring them to session one as a *fait accompli*. You get five loners with no reason to stay together.

**At Session Zero, build as a group:**

1. **Pitch concepts in one sentence.** Each player describes their character idea.
2. **Find connections.** "My ranger grew up in the border villages." "Oh, mine did too—were we friends?" Aim for at least **two pre-existing relationships per character**. Hostile bonds (rivals, ex-lovers, sworn enemies) create as much story as friendly ones.
3. **Tie backstories to the setting.** The DM should explicitly wire the campaign's central theme into each character's motivation *before* players run wild with ideas.
4. **No strangers in a tavern.** Decide why the party is already a party before play begins.

**The Campaign Questionnaire (5 questions, ~30 minutes):**
1. What is your name, species, and class? Who taught you your skills?
2. Where did you grow up or spend significant time? What was it like?
3. What is your personal quest or reason for being here?
4. How do you know at least one other party member?
5. What does your character want by the end of the campaign?

**The "Borrowed Detail" Technique:**
After backstory drafts come back, hand each player one specific detail from someone else's backstory—a name, a debt, a rumour—and ask for a single line connecting their character to it.

**The "Map They Draw" Technique:**
Bring a mostly blank regional map and have each player add one place from their backstory. By the end, the setting is half theirs, so it is theirs to have opinions about.

**Character Death and Replacement:**
- Is character death permanent?
- If a character dies, how is the new one integrated?
- Does the player sit out, or take over an NPC?
- How does the group handle the emotional response to losing a character?

### 6. Table Etiquette and Social Contracts

**Player Behaviour Agreements:**
- **Spotlight sharing:** How do you ensure everyone gets equal time?
- **Interruptions:** Are they acceptable? How are they managed?
- **Side conversations:** Allowed or discouraged?
- **Phones and devices:** Banned, allowed for D&D Beyond only, or free use?
- **Crosstalk:** How will you handle multiple people talking at once?
- **Rules disagreements:** Discuss at the table or after the session?
- **Food and drink:** Host rotation, dietary restrictions, alcohol policy?
- **How to raise concerns:** Is there a designated channel (private message, group chat, etc.)?

**PvP (Player vs. Player) Agreements:**
PvP is broader than just combat. It includes stealing from allies, lying to the party, sabotaging plans, charming party members, or creating a character who constantly works against the group.

- **Is PvP allowed at all?** (Most tables default to "no.")
- **If allowed, is consent required?** (e.g., both players must agree OOC before any PvP action.)
- **What about secrets?** Can characters keep major secrets from the group?
- **Betrayal policy:** Is inter-party betrayal ever acceptable? Under what conditions?

> **Critical Insight:** Without a stated PvP rule, a betrayal that one player considers dramatically appropriate feels to another like a personal attack.

**Metagaming:**
- How is player knowledge separated from character knowledge?
- What happens if a player knows monster stats or lore their character wouldn't?
- Is reading ahead in published adventures grounds for removal from certain plot reveals?

### 7. Scheduling, Logistics, and Campaign Tracking

Most campaigns don't die from bad stories. They die because nobody could agree on a night to play.

**Agree on:**
- **Day and time:** Start and end times. Be realistic, not aspirational.
- **Frequency:** Weekly, fortnightly, monthly?
- **Session length:** How many hours per sitting?
- **Platform:** In-person, Roll20, Foundry, Discord, etc.
- **Minimum notice for cancellation:** 24 hours? 48 hours?
- **Absence policy:** Do you play on with the character in narrative limbo, postpone, or run a sidequest?
- **Late policy:** How late before you start without someone?
- **Rescheduling protocol:** Majority vote? DM decides?
- **Record-keeping:** Who writes the session recap? Where do NPC names, maps, and decisions live?
- **Communication channel:** Discord, email, WhatsApp group?

**Campaign Tracking:**
Decide *now* where the campaign's agreements, NPCs, and lore will live. Options include:
- Shared Google Doc or Notion page
- Physical notebook
- D&D Beyond campaign notes
- Dedicated campaign wiki or world-building tool

> **Key Principle:** Handshake agreements about NPCs that live nowhere are the agreements that vanish.

## How to Introduce World Lore During Session Zero

Dumping encyclopaedic lore on players is the fastest way to kill enthusiasm. Instead:

1. **Start with the one-page pitch.** A single paragraph of flavour and a one-sentence hook.
2. **Give six truths.** Not sixty. Six things the characters know that make this world different.
3. **Use the "show, don't tell" approach.** Share a map, an NPC vignette, or a short in-character handout (a letter, a wanted poster, a newspaper clipping).
4. **Let players ask questions.** Answer what they're curious about. Don't answer what they aren't.
5. **Tie lore to character creation.** If a player wants to play a wizard, *then* tell them about the Academy. If they want to be a cleric, *then* tell them about the Church.
6. **Drakkenheim's approach:** Share the city map, the intro cinematic, and the spoiler-free player appendices. Let players discover the deep lore through play.

**Drakkenheim Content Warnings to Disclose:**
- Violence, murder, blood, gore, cannibalism, body horror
- Degenerative mutations causing physical disfigurement and madness
- Rats, spiders, insects, demons, undead, ghosts
- Natural disasters, large-scale loss of life, civil war, refugees
- Moral ambiguity, political conflict, religious zealotry, military nationalism

## Tying Character Backstories to the Setting

Sly Flourish's principle: *"The value of tying characters to the theme of your campaign during Session Zero cannot be overstated."*

**Techniques:**
- **Write the theme into the one-pager:** "Your characters work together with the Hellriders to protect and serve the city of Elturel." Players decide the particulars; they can't decide they don't care and wander off.
- **Loaded questions during character creation:**
  - "Why does your character want to stop the death curse?"
  - "Which of the other characters does yours trust, and why?"
  - "What is your character willing to risk their life for?"
- **Personal quests:** Drakkenheim provides 12 optional personal quests (e.g., find a lost loved one, reclaim a lost heirloom, join a faction, prove a royal claim). The DM weaves these into the narrative so the story is directed toward the city.
- **Faction relationships:** Ask each player to write one sentence on what their character thinks of each faction before play begins. These opinions can change, but starting with a mindset is invaluable.
- **The five-minute flashback:** Before a rulebook opens, play a short improvised scene for every pair of characters who share a tie. Half an hour total buys more investment than any conversation about backstory.

**The Three Vectors of Character Creation:**
1. Who the character is (the default focus)
2. How they fit the theme of the adventure
3. How they're bonded with other members of the group

Most players focus only on #1. Session Zero is where you enforce all three.

## Drakkenheim's Session Zero Structure

The Dungeon Dudes (Monty Martin and Kelly McLaughlin) explicitly structure their Drakkenheim Session Zero around these expectations:

1. **This is dark fantasy cosmic horror.** Expect horror tropes, dangerous monsters, and a ruined city.
2. **This is player-driven.** Choices and actions have consequences. Allies and enemies emerge from player decisions.
3. **This is morally ambiguous.** Good people do bad things; bad people serve selfish ends. Do not fixate on alignment.
4. **You cannot unite all factions.** You will eventually have to choose sides. Factions you oppose will send people to kill you.
5. **Encounter balance is not guaranteed.** Not every fight is winnable. Running away is valid.
6. **The Haze is real.** Contamination mechanics are core to the campaign. Understand them before play.
7. **Characters need motivation to come to Drakkenheim.** Create grounded, risk-taking characters. You don't need encyclopaedic backstories—just enough motivation to enter the city and interact with factions.
8. **Share the map.** Show players the city map in Session Zero. Familiarity breeds excitement, not spoilers.
9. **Personal quests are central.** Work with the DM to weave your personal quest into the narrative.
10. **Content warnings are provided.** Discuss lines and veils. The DM has final say on tone, but players should speak up about discomfort.

**Drakkenheim Player Prep (Before Session Zero):**
- Watch the intro cinematic
- Read Chapter 1 (spoiler-free introduction)
- Read Appendix E: The World of Drakkenheim
- Read Appendix F: Character Backgrounds
- Watch Part 1 of the Video Guide (Creating Characters)

## Starter Mission: Play Something Short

Many tables benefit from running a short mission *during or immediately after* Session Zero. A table that has fought one fight together is already a party.

**Guidelines:**
- Keep it small and low-stakes: one encounter, maybe two, 45–60 minutes.
- Nobody dies tonight.
- Give every character exactly one moment where their build or personality makes a call nobody else can make.
- End with a reason to keep travelling together: a shared enemy, a job offer, a shared debt, or an unfinished threat.
- Prep the enemy, the location, and each character's one choice; leave how they get there and whether they like each other open.

## After Session Zero: Capture the Agreements

Within a day, write a summary. By week six, everyone stops trusting their own memory.

**Document:**
- The campaign pitch in its final agreed form
- Lines, veils, and the safety tool in use
- Each player's stated preferences (combat vs. roleplay vs. exploration)
- Logistics agreements (schedule, absence policy, platform)
- House rules
- Each character's background and key relationships
- Where the campaign's lore and decisions will live

This becomes the campaign's foundational document. Update it when:
- A new player joins
- The campaign's tone shifts
- Someone's lines or veils change
- New house rules emerge

**Set a revisit date.** Check in at sessions 3, 6, and 10: "Is the campaign still delivering what you signed up for? Has anything shifted?"

## Printable Session Zero Checklist

### Before the Table Meets (GM Sends to Players)
- [ ] One-page campaign pitch written and shared
- [ ] Spoiler-free player handouts prepared (maps, world guides, cinematic links)
- [ ] Private message sent to each player asking about hard/soft limits
- [ ] Character-building rules documented (stat gen, allowed books, banned options)
- [ ] Session Zero date, time, and platform confirmed
- [ ] Safety tool chosen (X-card, Lines & Veils, Script Change, or combination)

### At the Table (Session Zero Itself)
- [ ] **Campaign Pitch** (10 min): Elevator pitch, setting, core truths, length
- [ ] **Tone & Genre** (15 min): Seriousness, comedy, horror, romance, references
- [ ] **Safety Tools** (15 min): Lines, veils, X-card, Script Change, open-door rule
- [ ] **Expectations & Play Style** (15 min): Combat vs. RP vs. exploration, optimisation level, backstory integration preference
- [ ] **Game Rules** (10 min): Edition, stat generation, sourcebooks, house rules, death/resurrection, levelling
- [ ] **Logistics** (10 min): Schedule, absence policy, platform, snacks, communication channel
- [ ] **Table Etiquette** (10 min): Phones, PvP, metagaming, spotlight sharing, interruptions, raising concerns
- [ ] **Character Creation** (30–45 min): Concepts pitched, relationships established, backstories tied to setting, personal quests assigned
- [ ] **Starter Mission** (45–60 min, optional): Short cooperative encounter to bond the party

### After Session Zero (Within 24 Hours)
- [ ] Written summary distributed to all players
- [ ] Lines and veils added to campaign document
- [ ] House rules posted where everyone can reference them
- [ ] Character backgrounds and relationships logged
- [ ] Campaign tracking tool set up (wiki, doc, notebook)
- [ ] Revisit date scheduled (e.g., session 3 check-in)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Treating Session Zero as character creation night.** Character creation is one part of Session Zero, not the whole thing. If you spend two hours on sheets and zero minutes on expectations, you've wasted the opportunity.
2. **Skipping safety tools because "we're all friends here."** Friends surprise each other. Safety tools aren't distrust—they're the mechanism that lets friends play intense games safely.
3. **Not writing anything down.** Verbal agreements evaporate. Written agreements become something you can return to when there's a dispute six sessions in.
4. **Running Session Zero as a monologue.** If you talked for 80% of the time, it wasn't Session Zero—it was a lecture. Ask more questions than you answer.
5. **Treating Session Zero as one-and-done.** Tone shifts. Comfort levels change. New players join. Revisit the agreements every few sessions.
6. **Letting players build characters in isolation.** A party of strangers who "meet in a tavern" gives you nothing to pull on. Build ties at the table.
7. **Being vague about the campaign.** "It's a fantasy game" tells a player nothing. Be specific enough that they can opt in or opt out with confidence.
