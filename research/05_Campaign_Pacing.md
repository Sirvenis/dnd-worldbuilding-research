# Campaign Pacing & Structure

Research on how to structure long-running D&D campaigns (levels 1–15+) into acts, arcs, and milestones. Covers structural frameworks, published campaign analysis, advancement systems, and practical session-to-session techniques.

---

## 1. The Three-Act Structure Adapted for TTRPGs

The classical three-act structure (Setup, Confrontation, Resolution) can be applied at three scales in D&D: the individual encounter, the adventure, and the entire campaign.

### Encounter-Level Three-Act Structure

| Act | Function | D&D Equivalent |
|-----|----------|----------------|
| Act 1 — Setup | Introduce the situation, characters, and goal | Room description, visible enemies, knowledge checks, goal stated |
| Act 2 — Confrontation | Rising action, complications, resource expenditure | Combat, skill challenges, dramatic roleplay |
| Act 3 — Resolution | Climax and denouement | Boss defeated, loot gained, consequences established |

At the encounter scale, the key is that **every scene must present a meaningful choice**. Dead space — scenes with no decision point — should be cut or summarized.

### Adventure-Level Three-Act Structure

An adventure spanning multiple sessions follows the same pattern:

- **Act 1 (Setup):** The party learns of the threat, gathers initial information, and commits to the quest. This is the "call to action."
- **Act 2 (Confrontation):** The bulk of play — exploration, battles, investigation, rising stakes. Obstacles escalate and an NPC may make a heroic sacrifice (the "plot point" that turns toward resolution).
- **Act 3 (Resolution):** The final confrontation, victory, and immediate aftermath.

### Campaign-Level Three-Act Structure

A full campaign (levels 1–15+) can be mapped to three acts spanning tiers of play:

- **Act 1 — Local Heroes (Levels 1–5):** The party discovers the initial threat. Stakes are personal or local. This act ends with the first major plot revelation or escalation.
- **Act 2 — Regional Stakes (Levels 5–11):** The threat expands. The party travels, gathers allies, and confronts lieutenants. Complications multiply and the true scope of the enemy is revealed.
- **Act 3 — World-Saving (Levels 11–15+):** The final confrontation with the true antagonist. Stakes are existential. The party must use every resource and relationship built over the campaign.

The campaign-level structure should include **dramatic turning points** at the boundaries between acts — moments that reframe what the campaign is about and raise the stakes.

## 2. Tier-Based Pacing (D&D 5e Tiers 1–4)

D&D 5e organizes character progression into four tiers of play, and these tiers naturally align with campaign pacing.

### The Four Tiers

| Tier | Levels | Narrative Scope | Typical Pacing Considerations |
|------|--------|-----------------|-------------------------------|
| **Tier 1** | 1–4 | Local threats, village heroes | Fast advancement; players learning their characters; short, contained adventures |
| **Tier 2** | 5–10 | Regional heroes, known names | The "sweet spot" of 5e; most play happens here; moderate advancement; faction politics begin |
| **Tier 3** | 11–16 | Continental/national importance | High-level abilities change the game; planar travel, legendary items; stakes are kingdom-level |
| **Tier 4** | 17–20 | Cosmic, multiversal threats | Few campaigns reach this tier; fights become apocalyptic; narrative must match mechanical power |

### Pacing Implications

- **Tier 1 should move quickly.** Many DMs advance players from level 1 to 2 after a single session. The Dungeon Master's Guide explicitly recommends this (Session-Based Advancement, DMG p. 261). Characters are fragile and options are limited; players want to get to their "real" abilities.
- **Tier 2 is the longest phase** of most campaigns. Under XP-based systems, Tier 2 actually slows down compared to Tier 1, while Tier 4 accelerates. This creates a natural "sweet spot" where players have options but not world-breaking power.
- **Tiers 3–4 demand escalating stakes.** If the campaign is still fighting goblins at level 12, the narrative feels disconnected from character power. The antagonists and locations must scale accordingly — demon lords, ancient dragons, and cosmic horrors.

### The "Scaling Spiral"

One effective campaign structure is the **Scaling Spiral**: each arc raises the scope of the conflict in a way that feels overwhelming but earned. Examples:

- Levels 1–3: Save a village from goblins
- Levels 3–6: Defend trade routes from a monster-backed bandit army
- Levels 6–9: Dismantle a thieves' guild in a metropolis
- Levels 9–12: Prevent all-out war between kingdoms
- Levels 12–15: Close a portal to Hell
- Levels 15–18: Battle across the planes
- Levels 18–20: Attack and dethrone a god

Each spiral should make the players say, "I don't know where you can go from here" — and then the campaign goes there.

## 3. Escalating Stakes & Major Plot Reveals

### When to Introduce Major Reveals

Major plot revelations should be spaced to maintain momentum without overwhelming the players. A useful rhythm:

1. **Session 1–3:** The hook and initial call to action. The "inciting incident."
2. **End of Act 1 (around level 4–5):** First major twist. The threat is bigger than initially understood.
3. **Mid-Act 2 (around level 7–8):** A betrayal, a lost ally, or a hidden truth about the antagonist's motives.
4. **End of Act 2 (around level 10–11):** The "crisis point" — the villain's plan advances, an ally is lost, or the party's failure has consequences.
5. **Act 3:** The final revelations before confronting the true threat.

### Managing Escalating Stakes

- **Start personal, end epic.** The most resonant campaigns tie the cosmic stakes back to the characters' personal journeys. The barbarian's village, the warlock's patron, the cleric's faith — these threads should converge in the finale.
- **Use the "Illusion of Choice."** If the party ignores the tavern hook and goes to the docks, the important NPC or clue moves to the docks. This preserves player agency while keeping the narrative on track.
- **Environmental prods:** When players stall or over-analyze, introduce an external event ("Suddenly, ogres!" or a messenger with urgent news) to force action without overt railroading.

## 4. Session-to-Session Pacing

### The Session Energy Curve

A standard four-hour session follows a predictable energy pattern:

| Phase | Time | Energy | Technique |
|-------|------|--------|-----------|
| Hook / Transition | 0–30 min | Rising | Start *in media res* or with a dramatic recap |
| Development | 30–120 min | High | Meaningful choices, exploration, minor obstacles |
| Mid-Session Pivot | 120–180 min | Dipping | Introduce a twist, revelation, or "Suddenly, ogres!" |
| Climax | 180–240 min | Peaking | Resolve the session's primary conflict |
| Wrap-Up / Hook | Final 15 min | Sustained | Deploy a cliffhanger for next session |

### The Three Models of Session Pacing

1. **Balanced Model:** Alternates action and roleplay. Ideal for urban adventures and sandbox exploration.
2. **Spike Model:** Centers on a single massive encounter surrounded by minimal build-up. Used for boss fights and set-piece battles.
3. **Escalation Model:** Steady ramp-up of tension. Ideal for dungeon crawls and horror arcs where rest is scarce.

### Practical Techniques

- **Start in motion.** Never open with "You're in a tavern, what do you want to do?" Open with a demand for action: "The door slams open. The town guard is here to arrest you."
- **Cut to the chase.** Skip mundane logistics (travel, shopping) unless they serve the story. Abstract them: "After a week on the road, you see the towers of the capital. What did you discuss during the journey?"
- **End on a cliffhanger.** The last beat of the session should be the sharpest reveal or threat. If the party is investigating a haunted mine, end when they *see* the ghost — not when they finish the entrance fight.
- **Recaps restore context.** A 3–5 minute recap at the start of each session is the single highest-return pacing habit. Use a running campaign log in third person.

## 5. Downtime Between Arcs

Downtime is essential for long campaigns. It provides:

- **Pacing relief** after intense arcs
- **Character development** opportunities (crafting, carousing, research)
- **World reactivity** — NPCs act, factions shift, consequences unfold
- **Player agency** — characters pursue personal goals

### Downtime Best Practices

- Use the downtime rules from **Xanathar's Guide to Everything** (Chapter 2) for research, crafting, and carousing.
- Frame downtime as a **montage**: "Over the next month, what does each character focus on?"
- Return the party to a **home base** every 6–8 sessions for a "nostalgia beat" — seeing how a familiar location has changed.
- Ensure downtime activities create **future hooks**: debts, rivals, new allies, rumors.
- Limit downtime to **1–2 sessions** or handle it between sessions to avoid momentum loss.

## 6. How Published Campaigns Structure Their Arcs

### Curse of Strahd

- **Structure:** Sandbox horror set in the demiplane of Barovia.
- **Pacing:** Open-world exploration with modular locations. Players can wander into dangerous areas early.
- **Key mechanic:** The tarokka deck reading randomizes key locations and allies, meaning each campaign has a different structure.
- **Milestone pacing:** Milestones are tied to specific accomplishments (e.g., escaping Death House, recovering a key artifact). Because the campaign is open-world, groups may level up quickly or slowly depending on where they explore.
- **Lesson:** Open-world campaigns need flexible milestones. The DM must be ready to adjust encounter difficulty on the fly.

### Storm King's Thunder

- **Structure:** A hybrid of linear prologue/epilogue with a wide-open middle chapter.
- **Act 1 (Nightstone):** Linear — a village attacked by giants. Levels 1–5.
- **Act 2 (Pick a Location):** The party chooses one of three settlements to defend from giants. Levels 5–6.
- **Act 3 (The Sandbox):** The most complicated section — a vast, open world where the party explores, does side quests, and eventually meets the frost giant Harshnag. The DM must connect the dots between locations. Levels 6–8.
- **Act 4 (The Oracle & Beyond):** Linear again — the oracle reveals the true plot, Harshnag makes a heroic sacrifice, and the party retrieves a conch to reach the giant court. Levels 8–10.
- **Act 5 (Maelstrom & Finale):** The party exposes the villain Iymrith, rescues King Hekaton, and fights the final battle.
- **Lesson:** Published campaigns can blend linear and sandbox sections. The open middle requires DM preparation to connect hooks meaningfully.

### Drakkenheim (Dungeons of Drakkenheim)

- **Structure:** Faction-driven sandbox set in a ruined city.
- **Pacing:** Personal quests form the core plot threads; faction conflict drives the world.
- **Key mechanic:** The city is divided into regions of increasing danger (Outer City, Inner City, etc.), with walls and gates that require faction relationships to pass.
- **Arc progression:** The campaign is at its most interesting when players are uncertain which faction to support. The DM should preserve that uncertainty as long as possible.
- **Lesson:** Faction-based campaigns create emergent pacing through player choices. The DM must make the world react dynamically to the party's allegiances.

## 7. Side Quests vs. Main Plot

### The Role of Side Quests

Side quests serve multiple pacing functions:

- **Pacing control:** They provide breathing room between major plot beats.
- **Character development:** Backstory quests give individual players the spotlight.
- **World-building:** They show the campaign world reacting to the players.
- **Resource management:** Optional content lets players choose whether to expend resources or rest.

### Structuring the Mix

- **One main quest, three active hooks:** A healthy campaign has the main quest plus 3–4 unresolved hooks (personal backstories, rumors, consequences) at any time. When the main quest stalls, activate a personal hook.
- **Side quests should connect.** The best side quests tie back to the main plot or introduce elements that pay off later. The duke the party saved in a side quest may become a crucial ally in the finale.
- **The "Hooks" page:** Keep a running list of one-sentence hooks. By month three, you should have 15–20 hooks you can activate whenever the players need direction.

## 8. Player-Driven Story Divergence

### Handling Divergence

Players will inevitably ignore hooks, kill important NPCs, or choose unexpected paths. Strategies:

- **The Illusion of Choice:** If the party skips the tavern hook, move the vital clue to wherever they go.
- **Modular prep:** Prepare locations and NPCs, not fixed sequences. The villain's plan advances regardless of player actions.
- **Dynamic world:** If the party ignores the cult, the cult succeeds in its ritual. Show the consequences.
- **The "Golden Path":** The narrative destination remains accessible even if the route changes.

### Failing Forward

If the party fails an objective, don't reset to the last town. Let the failure **move the story forward** in a new direction. The kidnapped noble is sacrificed, and now the party must stop the demon that was summoned. Failure creates new problems, not dead ends.

## 9. Milestone vs. XP Advancement

### Experience Points (XP)

**Pros:**
- Rewards player agency and proactive behavior
- Creates tangible, incremental progress
- Works well for dungeon crawls, hexcrawls, and combat-heavy campaigns
- Encourages players to seek out challenges

**Cons:**
- Slows down Tier 2 (levels 5–10) significantly
- Requires bookkeeping
- Can disincentivize non-combat solutions (if only combat grants XP)
- Under XP rules, reaching level 2 requires ~24 goblins for a party of four — often a slog

### Milestone Leveling

**Pros:**
- Story-led advancement; levels arrive at dramatic beats
- Simplifies bookkeeping
- Empowers the DM to control pacing directly
- Ideal for narrative-driven, roleplay-heavy campaigns
- Preferred by ~two-thirds of 5e players (according to 2020 polling by Sly Flourish)

**Cons:**
- Removes player agency over advancement pace
- Can feel arbitrary ("the DM says so")
- In open-world campaigns (e.g., Curse of Strahd), milestones may come too quickly or too slowly
- May disincentivize side content if it doesn't advance the main plot

### Hybrid: Milestone XP

The Dungeon Master's Guide (p. 261) offers a compromise: **Story-Based Advancement** (awarding XP at milestones). A workable formula:

- **Minor milestone:** Award XP equivalent to a hard combat encounter × 2
- **Major milestone:** Award XP equivalent to a deadly combat encounter × 2
- This results in roughly **3–5 major milestones or 10–15 minor milestones** per level.

### Recommendation

- **Use XP for:** Dungeon crawls, hexcrawls, sandbox campaigns where player choice of challenge matters.
- **Use milestones for:** Narrative campaigns, published adventures with clear story beats, groups with irregular attendance, and roleplay-focused tables.
- **Use milestone XP hybrid for:** Open-world campaigns where you want story-based advancement but players still want to see numbers grow.

## 10. The Five-Room Dungeon

The **Five-Room Dungeon** is a compact dungeon design framework that ensures a complete narrative arc in a small space. It is not about five literal rooms but five distinct scenes or challenges.

### The Five Rooms

1. **Entrance / Guardian:** A challenge that guards the dungeon but doesn't block entry. Establishes the theme.
2. **Puzzle / Roleplay Challenge:** A non-combat obstacle — a riddle, a trap, a negotiation, or an environmental hazard.
3. **Trick / Setback:** A twist — a red herring, a betrayal, a dead end, or a resource drain.
4. **Climax / Boss Fight:** The main combat encounter, often with the antagonist or a lieutenant.
5. **Reward / Revelation:** Loot, a clue, a map to the next location, or a plot twist that expands the story.

### Why It Works

- **Completes an arc in one session.** Ideal for the "7×4" framework (7 sessions of 4 hours).
- **Balances gameplay modes:** Combat, exploration, and social interaction each get a scene.
- **Scales easily:** Each "room" can be a single encounter or an entire session depending on the dungeon's size.

### Using Five-Room Dungeons in Campaigns

A longer campaign can be built from connected five-room dungeons, with each dungeon representing one "episode" in a larger season. A seven-session adventure arc might look like:

- Session 1: Setup and travel to Dungeon 1
- Sessions 2–3: Dungeon 1 (Rooms 1–5), ending with the first twist
- Session 4: Recovery, travel, begin Dungeon 2
- Sessions 5–6: Dungeon 2, ending with the second twist and pointing at the true villain
- Session 7: Final confrontation and resolution

## 11. Ending a Campaign Satisfyingly

### The Final Arc

The last sessions of a campaign should feel distinct from the rest:

- **Raise the stakes personally.** Tie the final confrontation to the characters' backstories, fears, and unfulfilled goals.
- **Make the BBEG a real threat.** Add lair actions, legendary resistances, environmental hazards, and minions. The party should feel uncertain if they'll survive.
- **Build anticipation.** Don't rush the finale. Add last-minute complications — a betrayed ally, a ticking clock, a civilian evacuation.
- **Let every character shine.** Give each player a mechanical or narrative moment that only their character can resolve.

### The Final Encounter

- **Combat-heavy campaigns:** The final fight should be the toughest in the campaign. Use all the party's resources.
- **Narrative campaigns:** A dramatic roleplay scene, a political standoff, or a world-altering choice can be as impactful as a boss fight.

### Closure

- **Wrap up loose ends:** Address major NPCs, the consequences of player actions, and the state of the world.
- **Player epilogues:** Let each player narrate their character's fate after the finale. The DM can embellish to make them more important in the world than the player suggested.
- **Return to the beginning:** Ending where the campaign started (the first village, the original tavern) creates powerful bookend symmetry.
- **Say "The End."** A definitive final scene, even a simple ceremony or farewell, signals that the story is complete.

### When to End

- **Before fatigue sets in.** Most campaigns face a "mid-campaign sag" around sessions 8–12. If the novelty has worn off and stakes haven't climbed, players disengage.
- **When the story is complete.** Better to end a campaign and start a new one than to let it fizzle.
- **When real life intervenes.** A planned, satisfying ending is better than a campaign dying to scheduling conflicts.

## 12. Key Takeaways for Campaign Pacing

1. **Structure at three scales:** Use three-act structure for encounters, adventures, and the full campaign.
2. **Match narrative scope to character tier:** Village → Region → Kingdom → Cosmic as levels rise.
3. **Keep Tier 1 short and Tier 2 long:** Most play happens in Tier 2; don't rush it, but don't let early levels drag.
4. **End every session on a hook:** Cliffhangers maintain momentum between sessions.
5. **Use short arcs inside the long campaign:** Think of the campaign as a TV season — 3-session arcs with their own villains and resolutions.
6. **Balance challenge with success:** Let players win often enough that failures feel meaningful, not inevitable.
7. **Choose advancement to match play style:** XP for exploration/dungeon crawls; milestones for narrative campaigns; hybrid for open worlds.
8. **Five-room dungeons are your building blocks:** They ensure every dungeon has a complete arc in a tight package.
9. **Preserve player agency with the Illusion of Choice:** The path can diverge; the destination should remain reachable.
10. **End with closure:** Let every character matter, tie up loose ends, and say "The End."
